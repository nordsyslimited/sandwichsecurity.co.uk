How to install a Ring battery video doorbell yourself

You can fit a Ring battery video doorbell yourself in about an hour. Charge the battery, set it up in the app, check the Wi-Fi at your door, then mount the bracket at roughly 1.2 metres and fix the doorbell with the security screw. The bits that catch people out are the signal and old brickwork.

What you need before you start

Ring's own instructions list a Phillips screwdriver as the main tool, plus a drill with a 6 mm masonry bit only if you are fixing into brick or stone. Everything else is in the box: bracket, screws, security screw and screwdriver for the faceplate. Add a pencil, a spirit level (a phone level app is fine) and a ladder only if your door is unusually tall.

Check your exact model's guide in the Ring app or on Ring's support site before you begin. Removable-battery and built-in-battery models are fitted slightly differently, and the steps below follow Ring's published order rather than replacing it.

Step 1: charge the battery and set it up before you mount it

Ring says to fully charge the battery first. On built-in-battery models you charge over USB, and some newer models use a USB-C cable that is not included in the box. On removable-battery models you take the battery out to charge it. Wait until the charge light shows full before moving on.

Then open the Ring app, choose to set up a device, and follow the prompts to add the doorbell to your Wi-Fi. Do this indoors, near your router, before anything is screwed to the wall. If setup fails on the bench it will fail on the door too, and it is much easier to fix at the kitchen table.

Step 2: test the Wi-Fi at the actual doorstep

Setup next to the router tells you nothing about the front door. Stand where the doorbell will go, with your phone on the home Wi-Fi, and see whether pages load quickly. Ring states that all of its doorbells and cameras work on a 2.4 GHz network, so make sure that band is switched on in your router. A doorbell that connects but drops out every evening is almost always a signal problem, and our guides on extenders versus mesh and on cameras that keep going offline cover the fixes.

Step 3: choose the spot and the height

Ring suggests mounting about 1.2 metres (4 feet) above the ground. That is a good starting point for seeing faces and parcels. Keep the unit clear of door furniture that blocks the camera, and think about where the motion zone will point before you drill. Our placement guide explains how to avoid catching the whole pavement.

If your door sits in a deep reveal or a corner, Ring sells angle kits (wedge and corner kits) that tilt the doorbell. Fit any such kit before the bracket.

Step 4: fix the bracket

  • Hold the bracket against the surface and mark the four corner holes through it, using a level.
  • On wood, Ring says no drill or anchors are needed. On brick or stone, drill 6 mm holes and push in the supplied wall anchors.
  • Screw the bracket on, hook the doorbell onto the tab at the top of the bracket and let it pivot flat.
  • Fit the faceplate and tighten the security screw. Use only the security screw supplied. Ring warns that a different screw can damage the battery.

Old brick, flint and timber in Sandwich

Much of Sandwich's centre is old brick, flint and timber frame. Soft lime mortar and crumbling brick do not hold small anchors well, so fix into a sound brick or a firm part of the door frame rather than a loose joint, and do not force a screw that spins. If your house is listed or in a conservation area, check with the local planning authority before fixing anything to the front of it, because a small hole can still be a consent matter.

Using existing doorbell wiring instead of the battery

Ring battery doorbells can also be powered from existing low-voltage doorbell wiring, but that is a different job from a plain battery fit. It involves the transformer and the chime, and in some houses those are fed from fixed mains wiring. Ring itself says to switch off at the consumer unit and consult a qualified electrician if you are unsure. Have it done by a qualified electrician or book us. Do not use more than one power source on the same doorbell, and if you use a plug-in adapter, set the chime type in the app as Ring's instructions say.

Quick checklist

  • Battery fully charged and doorbell added in the app before mounting
  • Wi-Fi tested at the door, 2.4 GHz band on
  • Height about 1.2 m, motion zone planned
  • Anchors used on masonry, no force on loose mortar
  • Only the supplied security screw used
  • Motion zone and sensitivity adjusted after a day or two

Common questions

Do I have to take the Ring doorbell off the wall to charge it?

It depends on the model. Ring says removable-battery models are charged off the bracket, while built-in-battery models charge over a USB cable. Ring also offers solar and plug-in power options, which need direct sunlight or a suitable socket. Check your model's guide.

Do I need to drill to fit a Ring battery doorbell?

Ring says wood needs no drill or anchors. On brick or stone you drill 6 mm holes for the supplied anchors. If you want to avoid drilling, Ring makes model-specific adhesive mounts, so check one exists for your doorbell.

Why will my Ring doorbell not finish setup?

The usual culprits are a weak signal at the point where you are setting it up, or a router that only presents a 5 GHz network. Ring states its doorbells work on 2.4 GHz, so check that band is on, move closer to the router and try again.

How high should a Ring video doorbell go?

Ring suggests about 1.2 metres (4 feet) from the ground. Adjust for your own door and the angle of approach, then tune the motion zone in the app.
